\begin{abstract} \noindent Indonesia's Village Midwife Program, launched in 1989, placed a trained midwife in every rural village as a response to maternal-mortality rates among the highest in Southeast Asia. This paper examines how exposure to the program in utero or in early childhood shaped human-capital outcomes measured two decades later. The 1984--1999 birth cohort of the Indonesia Family Life Survey is linked to the staggered community-level rollout, and the Callaway--Sant'Anna (CS) estimator with never-treated communities as the control group recovers the program's long-run effects on physical health, cognition, mental health, and socioemotional traits. Early-life exposure raises the adult physical-health index by 0.31 standard deviations, significant at the five-percent level, with effects concentrated in the in-utero and age 0--3 windows consistent with critical-period theory. Estimates on cognition and depressive symptoms are positive but imprecise, and the socioemotional-trait estimate is negative and marginally significant. A mechanism analysis of mother-reported pregnancy histories points to skilled birth attendance as the plausible channel. The findings suggest that maternal and neonatal health programs centred on skilled care at birth can produce measurable adult-health gains, but advancing cognitive and mental-health outcomes likely requires early-childhood-development services layered on top of the clinical bundle the village midwife delivers.\\

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} early-life intervention; Indonesia; village midwife; difference-in-differences; sibling fixed effects; human capital.\\

\noindent \textbf{JEL codes:} I18, I15, J13, O15. \end{abstract}
