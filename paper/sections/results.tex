\section{Main results} \label{sec:results}

\subsection{Headline estimates}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reports the effect of early-life village-midwife exposure on each of the four composite outcome indices. Column (1) uses the Callaway--Sant'Anna (CS) estimator \autocite{callawaysantanna2021}, which constructs the counterfactual for each treated cohort from the set of communities the program never reached and averages the cohort-specific treatment effects over event-times 0 to 3. Column (2) reports the analogous \textcite{dechaisemartin2021} estimator. Columns (3) and (4) retain the pooled two-way-fixed-effects (TWFE) estimator, without and with mother controls, as a comparison point with the earlier literature. Because the program rolled out in different communities in different years and treatment effects are likely heterogeneous across cohorts, the pooled TWFE coefficient is known to be biased \autocite{goodmanbacon2021}; CS is therefore the paper's preferred specification, and the remainder of the discussion centres on the CS column.

Column (2) is the corresponding \textcite{dechaisemartin2021} estimator. Columns (3) and (4) are the pooled two-way-fixed-effects estimator without and with mother controls. Sample is the non-migrant primary sample (cohorts 1984--1999). Treatment is exposure to the village midwife in utero or aged 0--3. The CS estimate on the adult physical-health index is 0.314 standard deviations, with a cluster-robust standard error of 0.159 and a two-sided $p$-value below the five-percent level. The de Chaisemartin--D'Haultf{\oe}uille estimate on the same outcome is similar in magnitude at 0.281 standard deviations but less precise, indicating that the positive health effect is not a feature of a specific aggregation rule within the staggered-DID-robust family. The pooled TWFE coefficient on the same sample is small and slightly negative ($-0.069$ standard deviations with controls), in line with the bias pattern \textcite{goodmanbacon2021} documents.

The CS point estimates on the cognition and depression indices are positive, at 0.189 and 0.044 standard deviations respectively, but neither is statistically distinguishable from zero at conventional levels. The socioemotional index is the one domain on which the estimate is not positive: the CS estimate is $-0.256$ standard deviations and marginally significant at the ten-percent level. Read jointly, the four estimates line up with the specific bundle the program delivered. The program's strongest and most precisely identified effect is on the margin the midwife's services most directly target, namely physical health secured through prenatal care, skilled birth attendance, and postnatal follow-up. Effects on cognition and depressive symptoms are positive but noisy. The negative estimate on socioemotional traits is unexpected and does not correspond to a channel the program was designed to affect; the multiple-inference correction in Table~\ref{tab:romano_wolf} attenuates its nominal significance.

Table~\ref{tab:main_results_by_sex} reports the column (4) specification estimated separately on the male and female subsamples. The sex split is reported on the TWFE baseline because CS does not yield a natural by-sex decomposition under the standard dynamic aggregation. Neither the male nor the female coefficient is distinguishable from zero on any of the four indices, and the sign pattern is revisited in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity} alongside the gender-specific findings \textcite{frankenberg2005} documented on earlier IFLS waves.

\subsection{Event study and pre-trends}

Figure~\ref{fig:event_study} reports the CS dynamic-aggregation event-study coefficients for the four primary outcomes over event-times $-3$ through $+4$. Beyond event-time $+4$ the cohort-village cells that identify the CS group-time ATTs become very sparse, because the program rolled out through the late 1990s and wave~5 observations occur in 2014; the displayed range is the window across which CS is identified off enough cohort-village combinations to produce stable estimates. The post-treatment coefficients trace out the same pattern visible in the aggregated column of Table~\ref{tab:main_results}. The health index displays a positive response to program exposure that emerges during the in-utero and age 0--3 event-time windows and persists through event-time $+3$, while cognition and depression show small positive post-treatment coefficients that are imprecise on a cohort-by-cohort basis. The socioemotional index trace is slightly negative across post-treatment event times, mirroring the pooled coefficient.

The pre-period coefficients largely straddle zero, with one exception: the health-index estimate at event-time $-3$ is $-0.33$ with a standard error of $0.12$, a two-sigma pre-period deviation in the direction opposite to the post-treatment effect. Event-time $-3$ corresponds to children born roughly four calendar years before a midwife arrived in their community, so the negative coefficient is consistent with non-random placement: provincial health departments prioritised communities whose baseline maternal and child health indicators were weaker, which mechanically depresses pre-program health for cohorts in the earlier-rollout communities relative to never-treated ones. A joint Wald test of the pre-period coefficients on the Sun--Abraham aggregation, reported in Table~\ref{tab:pretrend}, rejects at conventional levels for three of the four outcomes; the CS identifying assumption is the narrower parallel-counterfactual-trends condition between each treated cohort and the never-treated group, which the single pre-period spike at $t = -3$ nonetheless places under some tension. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} reports a \textcite{rambachanroth2023} sensitivity analysis that quantifies how much of a PTA violation the health effect can tolerate while remaining informative.

\subsection{Magnitudes}

The health effect of 0.314 standard deviations is substantively meaningful. The analysis-sample standard deviation of adult height (the largest weight in the Anderson health index) is approximately 8~centimetres, so an effect of 0.314~SD on the composite index corresponds to a change of the same order as a 2-centimetre shift in adult height, holding the other Anderson-index components constant. The magnitude is consistent with the range the early-life-environments literature has documented for comparable public-health interventions \autocite{duflo2001,maccini2009,ahsan2021} and substantially larger than the short-run effects typically reported for cash-transfer programs in low-income settings \autocite{premand2022}.
