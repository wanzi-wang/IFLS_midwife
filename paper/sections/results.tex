\section{Main results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Pooled estimates}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reports the main estimates of $\beta$ in
equation~\eqref{eq:twfe}. Each row is one of the four composite
indices; column~(1) is the bare TWFE, column~(2) adds pre-determined
controls, column~(3) adds province-by-birth-year fixed effects,
column~(4) reports the Callaway--Sant'Anna dynamic ATT averaged over
event-times $0$--$3$, and column~(5) reports the analogous
de Chaisemartin--D'Haultf{\oe}uille estimator.

The headline TWFE estimate for the health index is
\CoefHealthTWFE\ standard deviations (SE \SEHealthTWFE), statistically
indistinguishable from zero at conventional levels. Adding controls
and province-by-birth-year fixed effects barely shifts the point
estimate. The Callaway--Sant'Anna dynamic ATT is somewhat larger in
magnitude, reflecting the contribution of the never-treated comparison
group, but the standard error widens enough that statistical
significance is not achieved. This is a substantively weaker headline
than the predecessor DEPII analysis, which reported a marginally
significant point estimate of 0.055 SD on a more restricted
non-migrant sample.

The cognition index is essentially zero in every column. Bigfive shows
small positive point estimates of similar magnitude across
specifications, never reaching significance. The depression index is
the most consistent: a positive estimate of \CoefDepTWFE\ SD
(SE \SEDepTWFE) under TWFE---meaning fewer depressive symptoms among
exposed cohorts---that grows to about 0.10 SD with controls and remains
within standard-error bands of the staggered-DID-robust estimators.
None of the four indices is statistically distinguishable from zero in
any specification.

The pattern that does survive the multiple-testing imprecision of
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} is the gender split, reported in
Table~\ref{tab:main_results_by_sex}. Health-index gains are concentrated
among girls, replicating the central finding of the predecessor DEPII
analysis. The female-only TWFE estimate for the health index is
substantially larger than the male-only estimate, consistent with
\textcite{frankenberg2005}'s evidence that midwife services
disproportionately reach female infants and with the broader
literature on differential access to maternal and child health
services in South and Southeast Asia. The Big-Five and depression
indices show similar but smaller gender splits.

The post-treatment coefficients display the same pattern as the pooled
TWFE estimates: small positive effects on health (point estimates
around 0.1--0.25 SD across event times 0--7, almost always within their
own confidence intervals), near-zero effects on cognition and Big-Five,
and small positive effects on depression that fluctuate across event
times. None of the post-treatment dynamics looks like the steady,
monotone pattern that would be expected from a powerful treatment
effect.

The pre-period dynamics are less reassuring.
Table~\ref{tab:pretrend} reports a joint Wald test of the hypothesis
that $\delta_{-4} = \delta_{-3} = \delta_{-2} = 0$. The test rejects at
conventional levels for three of the four outcomes. The visual
inspection of Figure~\ref{fig:event_study} suggests that the rejection
is driven by noisy pre-period coefficients straddling zero rather than
by a systematic pre-period trend in any one direction; nonetheless, a
strict reading of the parallel-trends assumption is rejected. This is
one of the principal motivations for the within-mother design that
follows in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}: pre-trends in TWFE need not
contaminate sibling-FE estimates because the latter sweep out
time-invariant village confounders.

\subsection{Magnitude interpretation}

The point estimates above are reported in standard-deviation units of
the relevant Anderson index. To translate the depression-index estimate
into raw CESD-10 units, the cross-sectional standard deviation of the
CESD-10 sum in the analysis sample is approximately 5 points (range
0--30); a 0.07~SD effect therefore corresponds to about a third of one
CESD-10 point---small in absolute terms but on the same order as
short-term effects of cash transfer programs in similar settings
\autocite{premand2022}. The health-index estimate, which is a composite
of height (in centimeters), weight, and other measures, does not
translate cleanly into a single physical unit. A 0.1~SD effect on
the height component alone, by contrast, would correspond to roughly
0.7~cm. None of the headline point estimates is statistically
distinguishable from zero, so these magnitude calculations should be
read as an indication of what the data could reject rather than as
inference about a parameter.
