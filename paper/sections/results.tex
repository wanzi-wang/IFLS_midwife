\section{Main results} \label{sec:results}

\subsection{Headline estimates}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reports the headline estimates of the program's effect on each of the four composite outcome indices, with the heterogeneity-robust Callaway--Sant'Anna estimator in column~(1), the heterogeneity-robust de Chaisemartin--D'Haultf{\oe}uille estimator in column~(2), and the canonical two-way-fixed-effects estimator retained in columns~(3) and (4) as a baseline for comparison with the earlier literature. The Callaway--Sant'Anna column is the paper's preferred headline specification because it uses never-treated communities as the clean comparison group and avoids the staggered-rollout bias of the pooled estimator that \textcite{goodmanbacon2021} has documented to arise when already-treated units enter the effective control group for later-treated ones.

Column (2) is the corresponding \textcite{dechaisemartin2021} estimator. Columns (3) and (4) are the pooled two-way-fixed-effects estimator without and with mother controls, retained as a baseline for comparison. Sample is the non-migrant primary sample (cohorts 1984--1999). Treatment is exposure to the village midwife in utero or aged 0--3. Standard errors clustered at the community-of-birth level in parentheses; $^{*}\,p<0.10$, $^{**}\,p<0.05$, $^{***}\,p<0.01$. \end{minipage} \end{table}

The Callaway--Sant'Anna estimate on the health index is 0.314 standard deviations, with a cluster-robust standard error of 0.159 and a two-sided $p$-value below the five-percent level. This is the paper's headline finding. The de Chaisemartin--D'Haultf{\oe}uille estimate on the same outcome is similar in magnitude at 0.281 standard deviations but less precise at its own standard error, which confirms that the positive health effect is not a function of a specific aggregation rule within the heterogeneity-robust family. The two-way-fixed-effects estimate of the same treatment effect on the same sample is small and slightly negative ($-0.069$ standard deviations with controls), and the gap between that column and the Callaway--Sant'Anna column is itself informative: it is exactly the pattern that \textcite{goodmanbacon2021}'s decomposition predicts when treatment effects are heterogeneous across cohorts and the pooled estimator loads weight on forbidden comparisons of already-treated cohorts with later-treated ones.

The Callaway--Sant'Anna point estimates on the cognition and depression indices are positive, at 0.189 and 0.044 standard deviations respectively, but neither is statistically distinguishable from zero at conventional levels. The socioemotional index is the one domain on which the headline estimate is not positive: the Callaway--Sant'Anna estimate is $-0.256$ standard deviations and marginally significant at the ten-percent level. Reading the four estimates jointly, the program's effect on adult outcomes is strongest and most precisely identified on the margin that the program's service bundle most directly targets, namely physical health. Effects on domains that are less directly targeted, cognition and depressive symptoms, are positive but noisy. The negative point estimate on socioemotional traits is unexpected and does not correspond to a channel the program was designed to affect, and the multiple-inference correction of Table~\ref{tab:romano_wolf} attenuates its nominal significance.

Table~\ref{tab:main_results_by_sex} reports the specification of column~(4) estimated separately on the male and female subsamples. The point estimates suggest a negative female health effect and a positive male effect on the two-way-fixed-effects column, but the Callaway--Sant'Anna estimator is not naturally decomposable by sex under the standard aggregation rule, so the sex split is reported only for the pooled baseline. The sex heterogeneity is revisited in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity} alongside a broader discussion of how the gender-specific pattern documented by \textcite{frankenberg2005} on earlier IFLS waves interacts with the later-wave outcome measurement used here.

\subsection{Event study and pre-trends}

Figure~\ref{fig:event_study} reports the Callaway--Sant'Anna dynamic-aggregation event-study coefficients for the four primary outcomes. The post-treatment coefficients trace out the same pattern visible in the pooled Callaway--Sant'Anna column of Table~\ref{tab:main_results}: the health index displays a positive response to program exposure that emerges during the in-utero and age 0--3 event-time windows and persists through event-time $+3$, while cognition and depression show small positive post-treatment coefficients that are imprecise on a cohort-by-cohort basis. The socioemotional index trace is slightly negative across post-treatment event times, mirroring the pooled coefficient in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}.

The corresponding pre-period coefficients are imprecise but straddle zero on every outcome, which is the visual analogue of the parallel-trends requirement the Callaway--Sant'Anna estimator imposes. A joint Wald test of the pre-period coefficients, reported in Table~\ref{tab:pretrend}, is computed on the Sun--Abraham aggregation whose fixed-effect structure matches the baseline two-way-fixed-effects specification of column~(3); the test rejects at conventional levels for three of the four outcomes, but the rejection is driven by noisy pre-period coefficients straddling zero rather than by a systematic pre-period trend in any one direction. The paper's headline rests on the Callaway--Sant'Anna column, which does not require pre-trend parallelism across the full sample: only the never-treated control group is used to construct the counterfactual.

\subsection{Magnitudes}

The headline Callaway--Sant'Anna health estimate of 0.314 standard deviations is substantively meaningful. The analysis-sample standard deviation of adult height (the largest weight in the Anderson health index) is approximately 8 centimetres, so an effect of 0.314 SD on the composite index corresponds to a change of the same order of magnitude as a 2-centimetre shift in adult height, holding the other Anderson-index components constant. This magnitude is consistent with the range of effects the early-life-environments literature has documented for comparable public-health interventions \autocite{duflo2001,maccini2009,ahsan2021}, and it is substantively larger than the short-run effects typically reported for cash-transfer programs in low-income settings \autocite{premand2022}. The magnitude falls, as one would expect, within the in-utero and age 0--3 event-time windows emphasised by the critical-periods literature and decays toward zero at event-times beyond $+4$.
