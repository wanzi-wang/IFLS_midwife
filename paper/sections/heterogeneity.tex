\section{Heterogeneity} \label{sec:heterogeneity}

The pooled estimates of Section~\ref{sec:results} mask substantial heterogeneity across subgroups. Three dimensions are reported.

\paragraph{By sex.} Table~\ref{tab:main_results_by_sex} reports the main specification estimated separately on the male and female subsamples. The socioemotional-index estimate is significantly negative on boys ($-0.306$, SE $0.143$, $p<0.05$) and indistinguishable from zero on girls ($+0.011$); none of the other index coefficients on either subsample reaches statistical significance. The health-index estimate is $+0.023$ for boys and $-0.152$ for girls, the depression-index estimate is $-0.022$ for boys and $-0.111$ for girls, and the cognition-index estimate runs the other way ($-0.154$ for boys, $+0.039$ for girls). The sex split therefore does not recover a robust gender-specific health effect of the type documented by \textcite{frankenberg2005} on earlier IFLS waves and a smaller non-migrant subsample, and the lone significant cell, the negative socioemotional coefficient on boys, is one of four index outcomes per subsample and would not survive a simple multiple-comparison adjustment.

\paragraph{By maternal characteristics and region.} Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity} reports TWFE point estimates separately on six subsamples organized into three two-way splits: mother's years of schooling (median split at six years), mother's age at the child's birth (median split at twenty-six years), and region of birth (Java provinces 31--35 versus the rest of the analysis sample). The TWFE estimator is biased under heterogeneous treatment timing in the way Section~\ref{sec:results} documents and produces a negative pooled health-index point estimate of $-0.139$ on the full sample, so the subsample comparisons should be read as differences between cells rather than as standalone treatment effects.

The mother-education split shows a sharp negative on the high-education cell ($-0.454$, SE $0.186$, $t=-2.4$) against a near-zero estimate on the low-education cell ($-0.013$). The high-education negative is nominally significant at the five-percent level but should not be read as evidence that the program harmed children of educated mothers: the high-education subsample is roughly two-fifths of the low-education subsample, the cluster-robust standard error is correspondingly more than twice as wide, and mothers with above-median schooling in this cohort window plausibly already accessed skilled birth attendance and prenatal care through urban facilities before the village midwife arrived, leaving little scope for a program effect on their children's adult outcomes. The depression-index direction runs the way one would expect if maternal schooling and the village midwife operate as complementary rather than substitute inputs ($+0.158$ for high-education versus $-0.030$ for low-education), though neither cell is statistically distinguishable from zero.

The mother-age split shows a clearer gradient. The health-index estimate is essentially zero on births to mothers under twenty-six ($+0.027$, SE $0.126$) and turns marginally negative on births to mothers twenty-six and over ($-0.240$, SE $0.129$); the gap of roughly a quarter of a standard deviation is consistent with younger and first-time mothers benefiting most from a professional birth attendant, while older mothers with prior births rely less on the program. The same pattern repeats on the depression and socioemotional indices, with younger-mother estimates closer to zero or positive and older-mother estimates more negative.

The region split groups Java provinces and Bali (IFLS province codes 31--35 and 51) against the rest of the analysis sample, the latter spanning Sumatra, West Nusa Tenggara, Kalimantan, and Sulawesi. The health-index estimate is $-0.095$ on the Java-and-Bali subsample and $-0.200$ on the non-Java subsample, with the non-Java cell marginally negative ($t=-1.77$). The cognition and depression splits are essentially null on both sides. The socioemotional split shows a sign reversal ($-0.104$ on Java and Bali against $+0.054$ on non-Java), but both cells are noisy and the gap is well within sampling variation. Read as a whole, the substantive prior that non-Java provinces with weaker pre-program health infrastructure should benefit more from village midwives is not supported by the within-TWFE comparison.
