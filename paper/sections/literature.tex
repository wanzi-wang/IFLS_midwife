\section{Literature Review} \label{sec:literature}

\subsection{Early-life shocks and adult human capital}

The fetal-origins hypothesis introduced by \textcite{barker1995}, who traced adult coronary heart disease and hypertension to nutritional deprivation in utero, is the starting point for an empirical literature in economics linking early-life conditions to adult human capital. A full cumulative evidence is surveyed by \textcite{almondcurrie2011}, \textcite{almondmazumder2013}, and \textcite{currievogl2013}; the dynamic-skill-formation theory that motivates the focus on prenatal and early-postnatal exposure is provided by \textcite{cunhaheckman2007} in their review paper, which will not be explained in detail in this paper. The empirical work in this tradition uses two kinds of identifying variation: natural shocks (rainfall, in-utero fasting, weather, disease, pollution) and public interventions (school construction, vaccination, conditional transfers, early-childhood programs).

Three findings from this literature are relevant for this study. First, even one-time interventions can leave durable effects on adult outcomes. \textcite{atwood2022} exploits the 1963 introduction of measles vaccination across US age cohorts and shows that exposed cohorts have higher adult earnings and employment a generation later. \textcite{johnson2019} use IV and sibling-difference estimates to compare cohorts differentially exposed to Head Start and to K--12 school-spending policies by place and year of birth, and find that for poor children both increased educational attainment and earnings, with the Head Start effect amplified when followed by access to better-funded schools.

Second, the cognitive and anthropometric channels emphasized in the early literature do not fully capture the long-run effect of early-life conditions. \textcite{premand2022} report a randomized evaluation adopted by the Niger government cash-transfer program in which the addition of a behavioral-change promotion component improves children's socioemotional development without measurable change in cognition or anthropometric status; cash transfers alone produce neither. \textcite{sorrenti2025} exploit random assignment to a primary-school socioemotional-skills curriculum and document long-run effects on adult educational attainment that operate through reductions in attention-deficit symptoms and behavioral grades rather than through standardized test scores.

Third, sustained community-level interventions are structurally different from one-off shocks: they accumulate exposure over a child's developmental window rather than landing in a single year. The Village Midwife Program is in this latter category.

\subsection{Indonesia}

Indonesia is well-represented in this literature for two reasons. The most widely used panel dataset, IFLS, provides linked individual histories from childhood through adulthood, and the country has experienced a sequence of large early-life shocks and large-scale interventions across the panel's coverage. The work splits into three groups by the source of identifying variation.

The first uses cohort-by-region variation in policy interventions. \textcite{duflo2001} exploits the 1973--1978 INPRES primary-school construction program, which built schools in a phased rollout across Indonesian districts, and identifies substantial returns to schooling and wages a decade later. The second uses idiosyncratic in-utero or early-childhood shocks. \textcite{maccini2009} use rainfall variation in the year of birth as a quasi-experimental shock to early-life nutrition and find that women born in higher-rainfall years complete more schooling and reach higher socioeconomic status as adults. \textcite{majid2015} uses the rotating timing of Ramadan during pregnancy as a source of in-utero exposure to maternal fasting and finds reduced adult labor supply among the exposed cohorts. The third uses aggregate shocks affecting whole birth cohorts at once. \textcite{jayachandran2009} estimates that in-utero exposure to the 1997 Indonesian forest fires raised infant mortality, and \textcite{rosalesrueda2019} document persistent reductions in adult human capital among the same exposed cohort decades later. \textcite{giles2015} show that the 1997--98 Asian Financial Crisis produced sharp declines in child nutritional status, an aggregate shock whose timing partially overlaps the rollout of the Village Midwife Program.

Within this Indonesian work, \textcite{webb2024} is the most direct precursor to this paper. Webb uses early-life weather shocks among individuals born in rural Indonesia between 1988 and 2000 to test both cognitive and socioemotional outcomes in adulthood, and finds a sharp critical period for cognitive development at age two but no analogous critical period for socioemotional development; the effects on both margins are latent and emerge only after age fifteen. The asymmetry establishes empirically, in the same population the present paper studies, that early-life conditions can act on the cognitive and socioemotional margins through distinct mechanisms, so that the effect of an intervention on one margin cannot be inferred from its effect on the other.

\subsection{The Village Midwife Program}

Four papers evaluate the Village Midwife Program directly, spanning outcomes from birth to adulthood. \textcite{frankenbergthomas2001} use IFLS waves 1 and 2 to document positive effects of midwife arrival on women's body mass index and on birthweight, with significant BMI gains for women of reproductive age in communities that received midwives between 1993 and 1997. \textcite{frankenberg2005} extend this analysis to children's height-for-age and find positive effects concentrated among children whose mothers had access to the midwife during pregnancy. Together, these short-horizon studies establish the program's reach for maternal and child health during the rollout window.

Subsequent work examines longer-run impacts. \textcite{triyana2016} returns to the same cohort ten years after launch, exploiting the additional variation introduced by the Asian Financial Crisis-induced contraction in midwife stocks, and finds that the program's effect on antenatal-care use persists at the ten-year mark while most adult-health effects do not. \textcite{ahsan2021} extend the horizon further to cohorts exposed in utero and find gains in adult health, cognition, and economic outcomes among men, with antenatal-care receipt and skilled birth attendance identified as the operative channels.

Given these, two features of this prior literature motivates this study. First, past literature measure adult outcomes only on the cognitive, anthropometric, and economic margins; but mental health and socioemotional traits remain unexamined for this program. Second, all four studies on midwife program rely on pooled difference-in-differences specifications, which can be biased under treatment-effect heterogeneity in a staggered-rollout setting \autocite{goodmanbacon2021}.

\subsection{Contribution}

This paper makes two contributions. First, it extends the outcome set used to evaluate the Village Midwife Program to depressive symptoms, measured by the CESD-10, and to socioemotional traits, measured by the Big-Five Inventory; to the best of our knowledge, neither margin has been measured for this program. Second, the main specification uses the heterogeneity-robust estimator of \textcite{callawaysantanna2021} with never-treated communities as the control group, which addresses the staggered-rollout bias in pooled difference-in-differences specifications that the prior evaluations of this program have not corrected for.
