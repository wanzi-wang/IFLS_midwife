\section{Village Midwife Program} \label{sec:background}

\subsection{The 1989 program launch}

The Village Midwife Program (\emph{Bidan di Desa}) was announced in 1989 in response to a series of national maternal-mortality reviews that had identified the absence of skilled birth attendants in rural villages as the single largest contributor to Indonesia's high maternal mortality ratio \autocite{geefhuysen1999,gani1996}. Three structural features of the program warrant emphasis.

First, each midwife completed a three-year diploma at a provincial nursing academy and held full allied-health-professional certification, in contrast to the paraprofessional community-health-worker model adopted by comparable rural-health programs elsewhere in South and Southeast Asia. Second, midwives were posted as resident providers in the village they served and received a guaranteed government salary for the first three years of placement. Third, at the end of the contract period midwives were permitted to remain in private practice in the same community, with the contract window calibrated to allow time for a self-sustaining client base to develop on out-of-pocket payments \autocite{gani1996}. By 1996, approximately \num{54000} village midwives had been deployed \autocite{frankenberg2005}.

Three constraints shaped the pace and geography of the rollout. The flow of newly trained midwives was bounded by the throughput of provincial nursing academies, whose capacity expanded over the course of the 1990s. Deployment was administered by provincial health departments, which prioritized communities with the weakest baseline maternal and child health infrastructure: fewer health facilities, greater distance to the nearest community health clinic (\emph{puskesmas}), and higher baseline mortality. The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis (AFC) then disrupted the rollout. Fiscal contraction made the guaranteed-salary contract difficult to renew, and a portion of midwives whose initial three-year contracts expired in the 1997--2000 window left their assigned villages for urban private practice or family responsibilities \autocite{triyana2016,jointcommittee2013}. The contraction in the midwife stock during the AFC window introduced a second source of variation in village-level access to the program, but the data required to measure it precisely are too sparse to support a separate empirical analysis on the exit effect. This study therefore focuses on the entry shock. The subsequent exit of midwives does not threaten identification: health practices and clinical knowledge transmitted within communities during the years a midwife was present persist after her departure.

\subsection{Services provided}

Although skilled birth attendance was the program's stated rationale, the realized scope of services extended well beyond delivery care. \textcite{makowiecka2008} document the service profile in two representative districts: antenatal visits with anthropometric measurement and dietary counseling, postnatal home visits in the first month after delivery, family-planning consultations and contraceptive distribution, immunization referral, and primary preventive care for non-pregnant women and children. Midwives also distributed iron supplements to pregnant women, vitamin~A to postnatal mothers and infants, and oral rehydration salts to children with diarrheal illness. The breadth of this service bundle places the program closer to a general early-childhood health intervention than to an obstetric-only service, and motivates the broad set of adult human-capital outcomes examined in the empirical analysis.

\subsection{Non-random rollout and its consequences}

The prioritization of communities with weaker baseline infrastructure implies that the year a community received a village midwife, $\text{start}_c$, is mechanically negatively correlated with the village-level maternal and child health outcomes the program was designed to improve. Communities reached earliest in the rollout were the same communities in which a child born at any given calendar date would have faced the greatest health risk in the absence of the program. A simple cross-sectional comparison of early-treated to late-treated cohorts would therefore confound the program's effects with the underlying community vulnerability that drove placement.

Section~\ref{sec:empirical} addresses this confounding in three ways. First, community-of-birth fixed effects absorb all time-invariant village characteristics. Second, a within-mother comparison additionally absorbs every village characteristic that is time-invariant during the relevant cohort window, observable or not, by restricting identifying variation to siblings born to the same mother in the same village. Third, the main specification uses never-treated communities, those never reached by the rollout within the observation window, as the control group, avoiding the contamination that arises when already-treated communities serve as controls for later-treated ones.

These corrections do not eliminate the residual concern. Time-varying village shocks correlated with rollout year, for instance, the simultaneous arrival of other facility types or the construction of paved roads linking a village to its district capital, can still confound the estimate. The robustness check in Section~\ref{sec:robustness} addresses this margin with controls for the cohort-specific arrival of doctors and clinics in each village. The empirical design treats placement bias as severe by construction, relying primarily on the never-treated control group rather than on observable time-varying controls to recover the program's effect.
