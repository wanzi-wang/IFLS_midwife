\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

This paper examines whether early-life exposure to Indonesia's Village Midwife Program leaves measurable effects on adult human-capital outcomes a generation after deployment. Using the Indonesia Family Life Survey to follow the 1984--1999 birth cohort into adulthood and the Callaway--Sant'Anna estimator to handle staggered rollout under heterogeneous treatment timing, I find that children exposed in utero or before age three score $\CoefHealthCS$~standard deviations higher on the adult physical-health index and $\CoefCogCS$~standard deviations higher on the cognition index than children in never-treated communities, with both estimates marginally significant at the ten-percent level. The depression and socioemotional-index estimates are small and statistically indistinguishable from zero. The magnitudes on health and cognition are in the range other long-horizon public-health interventions have produced in comparable low-income settings.

The mechanism analysis points to village-midwife-specific delivery attendance as the plausible proximate channel: in-utero program exposure raises the probability of a village-midwife-attended birth by roughly $4.5$ percentage points on a baseline mean of $8.3$ percent, while leaving the aggregate rate of skilled care, prenatal check-up coverage, exclusive breastfeeding, and recorded birth weight unchanged. The pattern is consistent with the program shifting the composition of skilled deliveries toward village midwives rather than expanding skilled care into communities that previously had none.

This study generates two policy implications. First, a maternal and neonatal health program delivered at scale through a village-level cadre can generate lasting improvements in adult physical health and cognition in low-income settings, reinforcing the case for investments in the first thousand days.

Second, improvements in mental health and socioemotional outcomes are unlikely to arise from clinical care alone. Achieving gains along these dimensions requires complementary interventions, such as mental health services, parenting programs, or community-based psychosocial support, targeted directly at these outcomes.
