\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

Indonesia's Village Midwife Program, launched in 1989 in response to a maternal mortality ratio among the highest in Southeast Asia, delivered prenatal care, skilled birth attendance, and postnatal follow-up to rural communities the regular public health system had not previously reached. Two decades later, early-life exposure to the program shows up in adult human-capital outcomes on the margin the program's bundle most directly targets: the adult physical-health index rises by 0.31~standard deviations among children exposed in utero or before age three, with the effect concentrated in the prenatal and early-postnatal windows the critical-periods literature has identified as formative. The magnitude is in the range other long-horizon public-health interventions have produced in comparable low-income settings.

The mechanism analysis points to skilled birth attendance, the component of the service bundle the midwife was most directly trained and deployed to provide, as the plausible proximate channel; prenatal check-up coverage and breastfeeding initiation were already near-universal in the analysis cohort and therefore had no margin left for a marginal program to move. Effects on adult cognition and depressive symptoms are positive but imprecise, and the socioemotional-trait estimate is negative and only marginally significant. The narrow bundle the village midwife delivered, engineered to prevent maternal and neonatal mortality and to secure early-life physical-health inputs, was not engineered to reach the parenting, early-childhood-stimulation, or mental-health channels the broader child-development literature ties to cognitive and socioemotional outcomes.

Two policy implications follow. First, a well-designed, clinically-specific maternal and neonatal health program, delivered at scale through a village-level cadre, can produce measurable adult-health returns a generation after deployment in a low-income setting, which is a substantive addition to the case for investing in the first thousand days. Second, reaching cognitive, mental-health, and socioemotional outcomes at the same scale will require complementary programming designed to act on those margins directly, for example early-childhood-development services, preschool provision, or parenting interventions, rather than expected to flow automatically from clinical maternal and neonatal care.
