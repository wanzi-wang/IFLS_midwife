\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Indonesia entered the 1990s with one of Southeast Asia's highest
maternal mortality ratios. In 1990 roughly 450 women died for every
100{,}000 live births \autocite{unicef}, a rate driven largely by
unattended deliveries in rural communities where the only birth
attendants were untrained traditional midwives (\emph{dukun bayi}).
Mothers without access to skilled care were exposed to the standard
catastrophic complications of childbirth---obstructed labor,
postpartum hemorrhage, eclampsia---that are largely preventable when
a trained provider is present \autocite{geefhuysen1999,
makowiecka2008}. In 1989 the Indonesian government responded with a
national program, \emph{Bidan di Desa} (``Village Midwife''), that
trained and deployed tens of thousands of midwives, one per village,
across rural Indonesia. By 1996 some \num{54000} village midwives had
been placed; each served the village population for at least three
years on a guaranteed government salary, after which she could
transition to private practice in the same community
\autocite{gani1996}.

Because village midwives provided not only skilled delivery care but
also antenatal and postnatal services, family planning, nutrition
counseling, and supplementation of iron and Vitamin A
\autocite{frankenberg2005,makowiecka2008}, the program is
naturally interpreted as a bundle of early-life interventions. A large
literature in development economics has documented that early-life
shocks---in utero and the first three years of life---have effects on
human capital that persist into adulthood
\autocite{barker1995,duflo2001,maccini2009,majid2015}. The Village
Midwife Program is, in effect, a positive shock to the prenatal and
infancy environment for cohorts born after the midwife's arrival in
their community. Documenting its long-run effects on child
human capital is the natural empirical question.

\paragraph{Research question.}
Does early-life exposure to the Village Midwife Program have
detectable effects on adult cognitive, health, depression, and
personality outcomes? If so, are the effects concentrated in
particular domains, subgroups, or developmental windows, in ways that
discriminate among competing mechanisms?

The empirical setting has two features that help answer this question.
First, the rollout was staggered across communities over the 1989--2007
window, so the same calendar cohort can have different exposure
status depending on when its community received its midwife. Second,
the IFLS panel includes wave~5 (2014) outcomes for cohorts born in
1984--1999, who at survey were aged 15--30---late enough to observe
adult human-capital outcomes but recent enough that the youngest
cohort was potentially in utero when the midwife arrived.

\paragraph{Prior work and contribution.}
The closest predecessor papers are \textcite{frankenberg2005}, who
document a positive effect on children's height-for-age within ten
years of program rollout; \textcite{triyana2016}, who finds that the
effect on prenatal-care use persists at the ten-year mark but most
adult-health effects do not; and \textcite{ahsan2021}, who use a
larger window and report long-run effects of in-utero exposure on
men's adult health, cognition, and economic outcomes. The present
study departs from this lineage in three ways.

First, the identification stack is materially stronger than the
\textcite{ahsan2021} \texttt{areg}-with-community-absorbed design,
which predates the now-standard concerns about staggered-DiD bias
articulated by \textcite{goodmanbacon2021}. I report the canonical
TWFE estimator alongside the \textcite{callawaysantanna2021}
heterogeneity-robust estimator, the
\textcite{dechaisemartin2021} alternative, and the Sun--Abraham
event study. More importantly, the principal robustness check is a
\emph{within-mother} specification that exploits sibling differences
in exposure timing within a single household. Comparing siblings born
to the same mother in the same village before versus after midwife
arrival sweeps out every time-invariant village
characteristic---poverty, remoteness, baseline infrastructure---that
drove the non-random program rollout and that an inverse-probability
weighting strategy on baseline observables (the predecessor DEPII
analysis's correction) cannot fully address.

Second, I extend the outcome set in two directions that earlier work
did not pursue at this scale. Adult mental health is captured by a
standardized index of CESD-10 depression symptoms, the same instrument
used in the IFLS adult module since wave~3. Personality is captured by
a Big-Five personality index built from the fifteen Big-Five Inventory
items in IFLS wave~5. Both extensions are motivated by a recent
literature that has begun to document the long-run consequences of
early-life environments on socioemotional and psychological outcomes
\autocite{casepaxson2008,premand2022,sorrenti2025,webb2024,headstart}.
These outcomes plausibly capture program impacts that show up neither
in physical health nor in fluid cognition.

Third, the analysis is honest about identification weaknesses that the
predecessor literature has tended to bury. Pre-trend tests reject
parallel trends for three of four outcome families. Oster (2019)
sensitivity bounds indicate that the headline TWFE estimates can be
overturned by selection on unobservables that is small relative to
selection on observables. Reporting these diagnostics explicitly
strengthens the claims that do survive (the within-mother depression
estimate, the gender heterogeneity in health) by clarifying which
parts of the story are robust and which are not.

\paragraph{Findings.}
The headline TWFE estimates of the program's effect on adult outcomes
are small and statistically indistinguishable from zero across all four
composite indices. Pre-trend tests reject for three of four outcomes,
and Oster bounds suggest the estimates are fragile. Two findings
nonetheless survive the layered identification stack. First, the
within-mother specification produces a depression-index point estimate
of \CoefDepSibFE~SD (SE \SEDepSibFE), nearly twice the naïve TWFE
estimate---consistent with the program raising adult mental-health
margins, with TWFE biased downward by non-random placement in
poorer or sicker villages. Second, health-index gains are concentrated
among girls. The pattern of effects across age windows is consistent
with a critical-period interpretation, with the largest point
estimates in the in-utero and 0--3 windows.
