\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

Indonesia entered the 1990s with one of Southeast Asia's highest maternal mortality ratios. In 1990 roughly 450 women died for every 100{,}000 live births \autocite{unicef}, a rate driven largely by unattended deliveries in rural communities where the only birth attendants were untrained traditional midwives (\emph{dukun bayi}). Mothers without access to skilled care faced the standard catastrophic complications of childbirth, obstructed labor, postpartum hemorrhage, eclampsia, that are largely preventable when a trained provider is present at the delivery \autocite{geefhuysen1999,makowiecka2008}. In 1989 the Indonesian government responded with a national program, \emph{Bidan di Desa} (``Village Midwife''), that trained and deployed tens of thousands of young midwives, at least one per village, across rural Indonesia; by 1996 roughly \num{54000} had been placed, each guaranteed a three-year government salary and then permitted to continue in private practice in the same community \autocite{gani1996}. The midwives provide a wide range of health services for mothers and newborns. They monitored pregnancies, attend deliveries, visited newborns, advised on nutrition, distributed iron and vitamin A supplements, provided health knowledge, and ran the village's family-planning service \autocite{frankenberg2005,makowiecka2008}. As such, midwifery services constitute a direct intervention to mitigate adverse maternal and child health outcomes, serving as a long-term investment in human capital. 

This paper estimates the medium- and long-run effects of early-life exposure to Indonesia's village midwife program on adult human-capital accumulation. Identification comes from variation in the year a village midwife first arrived in each community: cohorts born in the same calendar year met a midwife at different ages depending on their community's rollout date, with the youngest cohorts exposed in utero and the oldest reached only after the critical-periods window had closed. Communities the rollout never reached serve as never-treated controls, and communities reached later in the rollout serve as not-yet-treated controls for cohorts in earlier-treated communities, together forming a clean comparison group. Outcomes are measured in adulthood at ages 15--30 across four human-capital domains: physical health anchored by adult height and biomarkers, cognitive function, depressive symptoms (CESD-10), and the Big-Five socioemotional inventory.

This study has three major findings. First, early-life exposure raises the adult physical-health index by \CoefHealthCS\ standard deviations (SE \SEHealthCS), with effects concentrated in the in-utero and age 0--3 event-time windows emphasized by the critical-periods literature. Second, the cognition index is also raised by \CoefCogCS\ standard deviations (SE \SECogCS), marginally significant at the 10\% level; estimates on depressive symptoms and on socioemotional traits are small and statistically insignificant, so the program's measurable long-run effects concentrate on the physical-health and cognitive margins. Third, a mechanism analysis of mother-reported pregnancy histories points to skilled birth attendance as the plausible channel through which the physical-health effect operates, the component of the program's service bundle a village midwife most directly delivers.

The findings sit between the more cautious intermediate-horizon estimates of \textcite{triyana2016}, who reported that most adult-health effects of the program did not persist ten years after launch, and the more optimistic long-horizon estimates of \textcite{ahsan2021}, who document in-utero-exposure effects on adult health, cognition, and economic outcomes among men. Relative to this literature, this study makes two contributions. It extends the outcome set to adult mental health and socioemotional traits, which the early-life-environments literature has identified as plausible long-run margins \autocite{webb2024} and which no prior study of this program has measured directly. Methodologically, this paper contributes by applying the Callaway–Sant’Anna (CS) estimator to estimate dynamic treatment effects under staggered program rollout. To the best of our knowledge, existing studies of this program primarily rely on traditional two-way fixed effects specifications, which may be biased in the presence of treatment effect heterogeneity.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews the program's institutional setting. Section~\ref{sec:literature} positions the study against the early-life-shocks, program-specific, and socioemotional literatures. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and sample construction. Section~\ref{sec:empirical} develops the identification strategy. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the main estimates and event-study evidence. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} reports the robustness suite, including the within-mother specification and the divergence between it and the cohort-level effect on the health margin. Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity} reports subgroup estimates by sex and mother's education; the gender split does not recover a robust gender-specific effect at the precision of the present sample. Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms} reports the mechanism analysis. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the findings and discusses limitations. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
