\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

Indonesia entered the 1990s with one of Southeast Asia's highest maternal mortality ratios. In 1990 roughly 450 women died for every 100{,}000 live births \autocite{unicef}, a rate driven largely by unattended deliveries in rural communities where the only birth attendants were untrained traditional midwives (\emph{dukun bayi}). Mothers without access to skilled care faced the standard catastrophic complications of childbirth, obstructed labour, postpartum haemorrhage, eclampsia, that are largely preventable when a trained provider is present at the delivery \autocite{geefhuysen1999,makowiecka2008}. In 1989 the Indonesian government responded with a national program, \emph{Bidan di Desa} (``Village Midwife''), that trained and deployed tens of thousands of young midwives, one per village, across rural Indonesia; by 1996 roughly \num{54000} had been placed, each guaranteed a three-year government salary and then permitted to continue in private practice in the same community \autocite{gani1996}. The midwives did more than attend deliveries. They monitored pregnancies, visited newborns, advised on nutrition, distributed iron and vitamin A supplements, and ran the village's family-planning service \autocite{frankenberg2005,makowiecka2008}. The bundle, arriving in a village that had previously had none of these services, is a natural candidate for a long-horizon human-capital intervention concentrated on exactly the prenatal and early-postnatal windows the developmental-medicine literature has flagged as formative \autocite{barker1995,duflo2001,maccini2009,majid2015}.

A generation later, what became of the children who met a midwife in the first three years of their lives? On the Indonesia Family Life Survey cohort born between 1984 and 1999, early-life exposure to the program raises the adult physical-health index by roughly a third of a standard deviation. Identification rests on the program's own rollout schedule: because village midwives arrived in different communities in different years between 1989 and the late 1990s, children born in the same calendar year met a midwife at different ages depending on where they grew up, and the communities the rollout never reached furnish a clean comparison group for the cohorts that did. Outcomes are observed in IFLS wave~5 (2014), when the study cohort is aged 15--30 and a rich set of human-capital margins is measurable: physical health anchored by adult height and biomarkers, cognitive function, depressive symptoms (CESD-10), and the Big-Five socioemotional inventory \autocite{casepaxson2008,webb2024,sorrenti2025,premand2022,headstart}. Following \textcite{anderson2008}, the outcomes are aggregated into four inverse-covariance-weighted composite indices, one per domain.

Three findings emerge. First, early-life exposure raises the adult physical-health index by 0.31 standard deviations, significant at the five-percent level, with effects concentrated in the in-utero and age 0--3 event-time windows emphasised by the critical-periods literature. Second, estimates on cognition and depressive symptoms are positive but imprecise, and the socioemotional-trait estimate is negative and marginally significant; these null and negative margins line up with the program's narrow service bundle, which delivered prenatal care, skilled birth attendance, and postnatal follow-up but was not designed to reach childhood cognitive or socioemotional development. Third, a mechanism analysis of mother-reported pregnancy histories points to skilled birth attendance as the plausible channel through which the physical-health effect operates, the component of the program's service bundle a village midwife most directly delivers.

The findings sit between the more cautious intermediate-horizon estimates of \textcite{triyana2016}, who reported that most adult-health effects of the program did not persist ten years after launch, and the more optimistic long-horizon estimates of \textcite{ahsan2021}, who document in-utero-exposure effects on adult health, cognition, and economic outcomes among men. Relative to this literature, the paper makes three contributions. It extends the outcome set to adult mental health and socioemotional traits, which the early-life-environments literature has identified as plausible long-run margins \autocite{webb2024} and which no prior study of this program has measured directly. It estimates the program's dynamic effects on adult outcomes under a design that addresses the staggered-rollout bias the pooled two-way-fixed-effects estimator is known to inherit \autocite{goodmanbacon2021}, using the Callaway--Sant'Anna (CS) estimator with never-treated communities as the control group. And it closes with a mechanism analysis that decomposes the reduced-form health effect into a first stage on three mother-reported mediators and a mediator-conditional effect on each adult outcome, which is the first such decomposition for this program using the pregnancy-history data the IFLS itself provides.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews the program's institutional setting. Section~\ref{sec:literature} positions the study against the early-life-shocks, program-specific, and socioemotional literatures. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and sample construction. Section~\ref{sec:empirical} develops the identification strategy. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the headline estimates and event-study evidence. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} reports the robustness suite, including the within-mother specification and the between-estimator disagreement on the health margin. Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity} reports subgroup estimates by sex and mother's education; the gender split does not recover a robust gender-specific effect at the precision of the present sample. Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms} reports the mechanism analysis. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the findings and discusses limitations. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
